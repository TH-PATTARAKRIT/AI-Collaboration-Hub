## 1. Explain Periodic accounting from purchase through closing and COGS.

Under Periodic, the financial and physical sides of the business run on two independent tracks that only meet at close. A purchase order creates no financial trace at all — it is a commitment. Physical receipt is likewise a stock-truth-only event: the goods arrive, quantity moves, but no journal entry fires. The first point cost becomes financially visible is the **vendor bill**, which posts an ordinary expense-by-nature entry — under Continental accounting, this is deliberate: cost is recognized as an expense when the vendor's claim is recorded, not when goods arrive or are sold. On the sales side, the customer invoice recognizes revenue and a receivable, but posts **no COGS entry** — cost recognition for the whole period's sales is not tracked transaction-by-transaction at all.

Everything is reconciled once, at the close: a physical stock count establishes the certified closing quantity and value, and a **Variation Account** absorbs the difference between what was expensed-by-nature during the period and what the physical count actually shows. COGS under Periodic is therefore not a transaction trail — it is a derived period residual, structurally the classic `Opening Inventory + Net Purchases − Closing Inventory = COGS` formula, operationalized through the Variation Account. The critical caveat I would not omit: that naive formula silently absorbs any scrap, shrinkage, write-down, or adjustment that was not separately measured and subtracted first, because the physical count alone cannot tell you *why* the ending quantity is lower than expected. This is the single most material correction this research produced (file `27` §5.2), and it is a direct, evidenced instance of the rule the whole session exists to prove: not every inventory-value decrease is COGS.

## 2. Explain Perpetual accounting from purchase through COGS and closing reconciliation.

I cannot give one answer to this question, because the reference ERP itself does not have one — this is the package's single most important finding. In the pre-major-version-19 regime, Perpetual posted a real-time journal entry at *every* physical stock movement: receipt debited Stock Valuation against an interim Stock Input account; the vendor bill later cleared that interim balance against Accounts Payable. On the sales side, delivery credited Stock Valuation against an interim Stock Output account; the customer invoice was documented as jointly clearing that balance into the true Expense/COGS account — though the reference ERP's own documentation contains an internal tension on the exact instant that clearing happens, which I hold as `PROVISIONAL`, not settled.

Starting at major version 19, the mechanism changed materially and is vendor-acknowledged as a deliberate redesign: the Stock Input/Output interim accounts are retired. The **Vendor Bill posting**, not physical receipt, is now the trigger that debits the Inventory Asset (Valuation Account). The **Customer Invoice posting**, not delivery, is now the unambiguous, directly-quoted trigger that debits COGS: "The expense account (COGS) is debited when invoices are posted." Delivery and receipt become Inventory-layer-only events; a Variation buffer account plus explicit accrual entries (Bill to Receive, Invoices to Be Issued) cover the gap between physical movement and invoice/bill posting. Even under this "at invoicing" model, period close is not eliminated — it still runs a genuine Stock Closing entry to catch whatever physical movement has not yet been matched by a bill or invoice, narrowing the close's role from primary posting mechanism (Periodic) to gap-filler (Perpetual). The teaching point I would stress hardest: a designer who studied only one version and called it "how Perpetual works" would be wrong for the other version, and SMEsPlus cannot inherit either one without an explicit decision.

## 3. Explain Product Category vs. Product Income/Expense inheritance and overrides.

The mechanism is a strict two-level cascade: if the product's own Income Account or Expense Account field is set, that value is used; otherwise the value configured on the product's assigned Product Category is used. This is a plain override, not a blend or a conditional precedence — when product and category values differ, the product's value wins outright for every transaction from the moment it is set. A third, journal-level fallback below category is repeatedly claimed in secondary community sources but was never confirmed against official documentation in this research, and an equally plausible alternative reading is that a fully-blank chain simply blocks posting until someone configures it — I hold this open rather than assume either.

Two things I would flag as more important than the mechanism itself. First, Costing Method and Valuation Cadence do **not** follow this same product-overridable cascade in the evidence gathered — they live only at category (and, in the 19.0 reconstruction, possibly company) level, with no confirmed product-level override; this is a real asymmetry against the Income/Expense cascade, not an oversight in my reading. Second, the single most operationally risky unresolved case in this whole precedence area is what happens when a product's category is *reassigned while it already carries stock* — no evidence anywhere confirms whether the reference ERP automatically re-classes the accumulated Stock Valuation balance from the old category's account to the new one. If it does not, every such reassignment risks stranding un-reconciled inventory value silently.

## 4. Explain why Inventory Value decrease is not always COGS.

Because a decrease in inventory value only becomes COGS when it is paired with a recognized sale — everything else is a different financial event with a different meaning and, properly designed, a different account. I can point to the reference ERP's own architecture as independent corroborating evidence for exactly this principle: it routes scrap, damage, and count-adjustment losses through a *distinct* location-type mechanism (an "Inventory Loss" location with its own configured Loss Account) that lands in its own labeled Profit & Loss expense line, structurally separate from Cost of Goods Sold — not as a naming convention, but as a different destination account entirely. Manufacturing raw-material consumption into WIP, and WIP completion into finished goods, are both asset-to-asset reclassifications for the same reason — value moves, but no sale has occurred, so nothing is released to expense yet. Thai TAS 2 states this same distinction as statutory law, not merely as reference-system behavior: ordinary cost-of-sales recognition on sale, write-down/loss recognition in the period it occurs, and reversal as a COGS *reduction* are three textually distinct recognition events, plus a fourth (re-allocation to another asset class, recognized as an expense over that asset's life) — never one undifferentiated "inventory went down." The important caveat: the reference ERP's own separation is **configuration-dependent**, not automatic — an unconfigured loss location has no documented fallback, meaning "loss defaults away from COGS" cannot be assumed unconditionally; it must be deliberately designed to be safe by default.

## 5. Explain COGS recognition timing and timing differences with physical stock.

This is the question `JT-04` exists to answer, and this research's contribution is proving that no single trigger event can be adopted from the reference ERP because it does not have one stable answer across its own version history — the same finding as Question 2, restated from the timing angle. Continental/Periodic ties cost recognition to the vendor bill (a purchase-side event, with no per-sale trigger at all). Perpetual pre-19 ties it, with some internal ambiguity, to something close to the customer invoice, following a delivery-time asset-side movement. Perpetual 19.0+ ties it unambiguously to the customer invoice alone, with delivery reduced to a physical-only fact. In every configuration examined, delivery by itself is never the direct accounting-layer COGS trigger — it is at most an inventory-layer event or an interim-asset-side movement.

The timing-gap control mechanism differs correspondingly: pre-19 uses per-movement interim accounts (Stock Input/Output) that clear move-by-move as bills/invoices arrive; 19.0+ uses a single Variation buffer plus explicit accrual entries, cleared in aggregate at closing. Both solve the same underlying problem — physical movement and financial recognition rarely happen at the identical instant — differently. The Thai matching-principle requirement (TAS 2: cost recognized when the related revenue is recognized) is the statutory constraint `JT-04`'s eventual answer must satisfy; it does not by itself tell us whether "revenue recognized" should mean delivery, transfer of control, or invoice for a Thai SME, which remains unresearched.

## 6. Explain returns and original cost linkage.

This is the single weakest and most material area in the entire research package, and I would say so plainly rather than paper over it. The reference ERP's own feature-level "how to process a return" documentation is completely silent on what cost value comes back into stock — the mechanical steps (reverse the transfer, then, if a bill or invoice already exists, separately create a credit note or vendor refund) are documented on one page, while the valuation answer lives on an entirely different, non-cross-referenced page. Where the valuation question *is* answered, the two cost methods disagree in a materially relevant way: under Average Cost, a return is valued at the **current** average at the moment of return, not at the cost that applied when the item was originally sold or purchased, and the average is explicitly **not** recalculated by the return itself. Under FIFO, the documentation is far thinner and only community-corroborated for how a return interacts with existing cost layers.

Worse, the reference ERP's own documentation admits a real, unresolved discrepancy: a return's inventory-value reversal is computed independently of whatever amount the matching credit note actually reverses, and when the two do not match — which is common, since prices and costs move independently — its own stated remedy is "manual adjustment." For a later-period return, three dates can be set independently (the original sale, the physical return, and the credit-note reversal date), with nothing forcing them into alignment. My teaching point: SMEsPlus cannot treat "process a return" as one atomic event with one obvious cost basis. Whatever `JT-05`/`C-03` eventually decides, the design must carry an explicit, traceable reference back to the original sale's cost-release fact and let Accounting — never Inventory — decide whether to reverse at original cost, current cost, or a defined blend.

## 7. Explain late supplier bill / landed cost after stock sale.

A late supplier bill and a late landed-cost allocation are the same underlying problem — a cost becomes known after the related stock's fate is already partly or fully determined — and the reference ERP handles them with real, if imperfect, mechanisms. For landed cost arriving after only *part* of the affected quantity has sold, the documented (though only community-corroborated) behavior splits the additional cost proportionally: the portion attributable to remaining on-hand stock capitalizes normally; the portion attributable to already-sold quantity routes to an expense/cost-of-revenue-type account instead, because there is no asset left to attach it to. For landed cost arriving after the *entire* quantity has sold, the evidence is genuinely inconsistent across sources — one description says the full amount books straight to COGS; a separate, more recent description attributed to the current major version says it instead requires a manually-generated entry through a dedicated clearing account for month-end reconciliation; and at least one reported case describes no journal entry being generated at all for this condition, which would itself be a control failure (a valuation change with no corresponding posting). I hold this as the package's single most material scenario-level finding precisely because it combines version inconsistency, a real control-break risk, and direct exposure to two open Joint decisions (`JT-04`, `JT-08`) at once.

For a late supplier bill more generally, I found no mechanism anywhere in the evidence that attributes the late cost back to the *specific prior period's* COGS it should have affected — it is simply absorbed into the current period's figures at the next close. Whether that is acceptable, or whether SMEsPlus needs prior-period attribution, is squarely `JT-06`'s question and is not answered by the reference ERP's own behavior, which does not appear to answer it either.

## 8. Explain Standard / Average / FIFO differences and migration implications.

Standard Cost holds one fixed, manually-set figure per product; every movement, in either direction, uses that same figure, and any gap between it and the actual vendor-billed price is diverted to a dedicated Price Difference Account rather than disturbing the inventory carrying value — this is the method's defining accounting attraction under Perpetual, because variance never distorts COGS. Average Cost recalculates a single running weighted-average figure on every qualifying receipt, blending old and new cost together, and deliveries draw at whatever the average is at that moment — meaning COGS under AVCO never exactly matches any one receipt's actual cost. FIFO retains each receipt as its own discrete, ordered cost layer and consumes the oldest surviving layer first on issue, giving the most granular, audit-traceable actual-cost figure of the three, at the highest bookkeeping cost — including a well-evidenced negative-stock mechanism where an over-issued quantity is valued at an estimate and automatically trued-up when a compensating receipt later arrives.

For migration, the demands scale directly with each method's granularity: Standard Cost needs only a single opening cost per product; Average Cost needs one opening weighted-average per product (scope of "per what" — company, location — is not fully confirmed); FIFO needs a full, correctly-ordered opening layer history, because a single blended opening value is structurally insufficient for a method that consumes layers in arrival order. Across all three, one consistent finding matters most for migration: a cost-method change does not retroactively rebase existing stock value in the direction I could confirm (moving away from Standard Cost) — existing units keep their old value, and only forward movement adopts the new method. The reverse and lateral directions (into or between AVCO and FIFO) remain unconfirmed and must not be assumed to behave the same way.

## 9. Explain Balance Sheet Inventory vs. P&L COGS reconciliation.

The reference ERP's own evidence treats this reconciliation as something that must be actively *produced* by a closing procedure, not something that holds automatically at every point in time — and this is true under both accounting patterns, for different reasons. Under Periodic, the two sides are *expected* to diverge continuously between closes by design, because the Balance Sheet inventory figure is never touched transaction-by-transaction at all; it only moves at the close. Under Perpetual, the two sides track more closely, but even the 19.0+ "at invoicing" regime still runs a genuine Stock Closing entry specifically because invoice-level posting is not guaranteed to coincide with physical movement — the closing entry exists to make the Inventory subledger and the General Ledger agree, which is itself proof they are expected to disagree between closes, not proof they never do.

The identity I would state carefully, with its scope condition attached rather than as an unqualified claim, is: *Inventory valuation as-of-date reconciles to the Accounting inventory balance, plus a fully explained set of reconciling items* — typical items being unposted landed-cost allocations, receipts recorded physically but not yet billed (or the reverse), deliveries recorded physically but not yet invoiced (or the reverse), and any costing-method change mid-period without a documented conversion adjustment. Any SMEsPlus as-of-date reconciliation report must state, as part of its own output, whether it is measuring at a closing boundary or at an arbitrary intra-period timestamp — an unqualified "these two numbers should always match" claim is not supported by any evidence this research gathered.

## 10. State exactly what remains UNKNOWN / HOLD and what the Joint Team must not assume.

In priority order, the Joint Team must not assume:

1. **That "Perpetual" means one thing.** It does not, across the reference ERP's own version history — see Questions 2 and 5. Any citation of "reference-ERP Perpetual behavior" must name which regime it means.
2. **That a customer return has one obvious cost basis.** It does not — see Question 6. This is the package's single most material carried-forward item, tracked as `JT-05`/`C-03`.
3. **That reassigning a product's category with existing stock is safe.** No evidence confirms the reference ERP re-classes accumulated stock value on reassignment; documentation-only research cannot resolve this — it needs a live-instance test.
4. **That the naive `Opening + Purchases − Closing = COGS` formula is safe to use as-is.** It silently absorbs scrap/loss/write-down unless those are separately measured first — see Question 1.
5. **That a landed cost or price-difference amount always posts somewhere.** In at least one reported case for fully-sold stock, no journal entry generates at all — a control break, not merely an open design question.
6. **That "loss is not COGS" holds unconditionally.** It holds only when a distinct Inventory Loss location and account are deliberately configured; no fallback for an unconfigured loss was found.
